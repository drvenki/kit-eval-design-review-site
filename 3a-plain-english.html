Statistics

In plain English

The whole statistical argument with no statistics in it. Written to be read by co-authors, and to be handed to anyone who asks why the figures changed and why we did not simply do what was suggested in the meeting.

Start with what actually happened at the bench

For each kit we made nine libraries. But we did not make nine libraries nine separate times. We went into the lab on three occasions, and on each occasion we made three libraries side by side.

Those three side-by-side libraries share almost everything. Same day. Same person. Same tube of master mix. Same plate. Same thermocycler run. Same capture pool afterwards. The only thing that differs between them is which well they sat in.

So what do the three side-by-side libraries actually tell you?

They tell you how precisely we can pipette. They do not tell you, three separate times, how well the kit works. If the enzyme was slightly past its best that morning, or the room was warm, or the bead cleanup ran a minute long, all three move together in the same direction. They are three views of one event, not three events.

The honest count of independent evidence per kit is therefore three, not nine. Three occasions. In statistical language, the "experimental unit" is the preparation batch, not the library. That is precisely the point ST made, and it is correct.

Why the current figures overstate their case

The test currently printed on the figures (Kruskal-Wallis, with Dunn for the pairwise comparisons) was handed nine numbers per kit and told they were nine independent measurements. It believed us.

Every statistical test works the same way underneath. It compares how far apart the kits are against how much the numbers naturally bounce around, and it becomes more confident the more independent measurements you give it. Give it nine when you really have about five, and it becomes more confident than the data justify. The p-values it prints come out too small.

That is what "anticonservative" means. It is not a rounding problem. It means the figures are claiming a level of certainty the experiment cannot support, and some of the differences currently marked as significant may be nothing more than the difference between a Tuesday and a Thursday.

And we can measure exactly how bad it is

There is a number for "how much of the wobble is day-to-day rather than well-to-well". Across our 21 kit and sample-type combinations it averages about half. For KAPA HyperPrep on cfDNA it is 0.98, meaning the three wells on a given day are nearly identical to each other while the three days differ substantially. That is AL's observation ("sometimes KAPA just gives poorer results") appearing as a number: batch 2 came in at 0.20 while batches 1 and 3 were at 0.32 and 0.34.

Working through the arithmetic, nine libraries per kit carry about as much information as 4.7 genuinely independent ones. So the current analysis is running on roughly half the evidence it thinks it has.

None of this means the study is wrong. The kits really do differ. It means the current p-values are not defensible, and a statistical reviewer will say so.

The obvious fix, and why the literal version collapses

The suggestion from the meeting was: average the three side-by-side libraries into one number per occasion, so each kit becomes three points instead of nine, then run the same kind of test as before. The first half of that is exactly right. The second half is where it falls apart, and it is worth understanding why, because the reason is not a matter of opinion.

What the current test does with your numbers

Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn are rank tests. A rank test deliberately throws away the actual values and keeps only the ordering. It does not care that one kit converted 42 % and another converted 24 %; it only cares that the first was higher. This is normally a virtue, because it makes no assumptions about the shape of the data.

But it means the only evidence a rank test can ever use is how the points interleave.

Now count what is available with three points each

Suppose we compare two kits, each with three batch averages. Line all six numbers up from lowest to highest and ask the only question a rank test can ask: did one kit's three points land above the other kit's three points?

There are exactly 20 possible ways to arrange three A's and three B's in six positions. Only one of those twenty is "all three A's above all three B's". So if the kits were truly identical and we shuffled at random, we would see perfect separation one time in twenty, which is a probability of 0.05 in one direction, or 0.1 counting both directions.

This is the part that matters

0.1 is the best possible p-value from that comparison. Not the typical one. The best one, achieved when the separation is perfect and every single point of one kit beats every single point of the other. If one kit were a hundred times better than the other, with no overlap whatsoever, the answer would still be 0.1.

And 0.1 is already above the 0.05 threshold before we have done anything else. Then, because we make fifteen kit comparisons within cfDNA, we have to correct for multiple testing, which makes every p-value larger still. Nothing can survive. That is why the naive version returns zero significant results out of fifteen in cfDNA, and it would return zero no matter what the kits did.

So the distinction to hold on to is this. A conservative test is cautious: it demands strong evidence, but if the effect is big enough it will still find it. This is not conservative. It is a ruler marked only in metres being used to measure the width of a finger. The instrument has run out of resolution before it ever looks at the sample. We would not be reporting "we looked carefully and found nothing"; we would be reporting "we used a method that could not have found anything".

A technical footnote for completeness: the software will sometimes still print a number below 0.1, because Dunn's test uses a mathematical shortcut that assumes larger groups than we have. Those smaller numbers are artefacts of the shortcut, not evidence. The exact calculation above is the honest limit.

The repair: borrow the noise estimate, not the answer

Here is the thing the naive version wastes. When we compare two kits using only their own three points each, we are also forced to work out "how much do these numbers normally bounce around from day to day?" from those same six numbers. Six numbers is a terrible basis for that judgement.

Think of estimating someone's typical commute time from three mornings. You get a number, but you have no idea how reliable it is, so you are forced to be extremely cautious about concluding anything from a fourth morning that looks unusual.

The insight

Day-to-day wobble is mostly a property of the process, not of the individual kit. Preparing a sequencing library on a Tuesday rather than a Thursday introduces roughly the same amount of variation whichever kit is in the tube.

So instead of asking "how much does KAPA bounce around between days?" using KAPA's three numbers alone, we ask "how much do library preparations in this lab bounce around between days?" using all five or six kits' numbers together, which is fifteen to eighteen numbers.

Now the estimate of the background noise is solid, and because it is solid we no longer have to be paranoid about it. Real differences become detectable again. This is the same reasoning as judging whether a runner improved: three of their own races barely tell you their normal variability, but if you know the typical race-to-race variability for that event, you can say confidently that a two second improvement is real.

In the technical write-up this shows up as "degrees of freedom going from 4 to 10 or 12". Degrees of freedom is simply a count of how much independent information you have for estimating that background noise. Comparing two kits in isolation on three batches each leaves about 4. Pooling across a whole sample type takes it to 10 with five kits, or 12 with the six in cfDNA. Going from 4 to 12 is the difference between a shaky estimate and a trustworthy one, and it is the entire reason the corrected analysis recovers most of the lost power.

The model doing this bookkeeping is called a mixed model, because it holds two kinds of variation at once: the kit differences we care about, and the batch-to-batch variation we have to account for but are not interested in. It also copes correctly with the fact that a few groups have eight libraries rather than nine, which a plain average-then-test approach quietly mishandles.

What this does not do

It does not smuggle the nine libraries back in

This is the question ST should ask, and the answer is clean. The comparison between kits is performed on the three batch averages, exactly as requested. "Three points become one point" is honoured precisely. The nine libraries contribute one thing only: they tell us how precisely each of those three averages was measured. They never act as independent evidence about the kit.

We verified this arithmetically. The full model gives F = 8.73 for cfDNA. Simply averaging to three points and running a plain analysis of variance on those three points gives F = 8.70. They are the same test. The model is just the bookkeeping that also lets us report the batch effect rather than hide it.

Saying so is better than being caught having claimed otherwise.

Finally, the assumption question that was raised

The meeting note said aggregating is acceptable "if we assume there is no difference between the operators or the dates". Those two are worth separating, because they have different answers.

Status
Dates We no longer need to assume this. The corrected analysis does not assume the days are interchangeable; it measures how much the days differ, uses that as the yardstick, and prints it on the figure. ST's concern that "we cannot assume complete independence for all kits" is not brushed aside, it is the quantity the method is built around.
Operators This one is a genuine and permanent limitation, and no choice of statistical test changes it. CDM prepared EVO and Watchmaker; VK prepared the rest. Because no kit was prepared by both, there is no comparison anywhere in this dataset that could separate an operator effect from a kit effect. We cannot test it, so we should state it plainly and show the operator-to-operator reproducibility data from routine production in the supplement, without waiting for a reviewer to ask. Detail

The one-paragraph version, if you need to summarise it in a meeting

We have three real experiments per kit, not nine, so the current p-values are too optimistic and must change. But averaging to three points and re-running the same rank test cannot work: with three versus three, the best achievable p-value is 0.1 even for a perfect result, so nothing could ever come out significant. The fix is to compare the three batch averages, exactly as asked, while estimating the day-to-day noise from all the kits together instead of from each pair separately. That is a standard mixed model, it gives mathematically the same comparison as averaging to three points, and it restores the ability to detect the differences that are genuinely there.
