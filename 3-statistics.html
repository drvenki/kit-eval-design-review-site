Statistics

The statistical problem, quantified

Your supervisor's observation ("some kits clearly have batch effects, which means we cannot assume complete independence for all kits") is correct and measurable. Here is the measurement.

What the current analysis does

ggstatsplot::ggbetweenstats(type = "np", ...) is a Kruskal–Wallis test on all libraries with a Dunn post-hoc. With 3 batches × 3 replicates it treats 9 libraries as 9 independent observations. They are not: the three replicates within a batch share a prep day, an operator, a master mix, a plate and a hybridisation pool.

The ICC is not merely non-zero, it is wildly heterogeneous, running from 0.00 (EVO FFPE 150 ng) to 0.98 (KAPAON cfDNA). AL's comment that "sometimes KAPA just gives poorer results" is visible in the data as KAPAON cfDNA batch 2 sitting at 0.20 while batches 1 and 3 sit at 0.32 to 0.34. The current p-values are anticonservative by roughly the design effect in variance terms, and by considerably more than that in the tails, which is where p-values live.

The key result of this review

ST is right that the unit of analysis must be the batch. But collapsing to three points and re-running a rank test would destroy the study. A Wilcoxon or Dunn comparison of 3 versus 3 has a minimum attainable p of 0.1 before any multiplicity correction, so it is not merely conservative, it is structurally incapable of producing a significant pairwise result. Option (b) loses every cfDNA pairwise finding, and it would lose them no matter how large the true effect. That is not a defensible place to land.

Option (c) honours the aggregation and recovers the power, because it pools the batch-to-batch variance across the five or six kits within a sample type instead of estimating it separately for each pair. Error degrees of freedom go from 4 to 10 or 12. The model and its justification →
